Who Were They?

The Women’s Movement, also known as second-wave feminism, was a social and political movement that became most active during the 1960s and 1970s. Feminists fought for equality in education, employment, politics, and society.

Women were often expected to stay home, raise children, and depend financially on men instead of pursuing careers or leadership positions. Many women believed society treated them unfairly and denied them equal opportunities.

“The problem that has no name” — Betty Friedan, The Feminine Mystique (1963)

This quote from Betty Friedan’s book became one of the most important primary source documents of the movement. Friedan described the unhappiness many middle-class women experienced while being told they should feel fulfilled only as housewives. The book inspired thousands of women to challenge traditional gender roles and demand equality.

Goals of the Movement

  • Equal pay for equal work
  • Equal opportunities in careers and education
  • Protection from discrimination based on sex
  • Reproductive rights and access to birth control
  • Greater political representation for women
  • Improved treatment for women of color

Women often faced discrimination in the workplace. Many jobs were unavailable to women, and those who worked were frequently paid less than men.

Women of color faced additional racial discrimination. Black women were commonly limited to low-paying service jobs and had fewer educational opportunities.

“There can be no liberation for half the human race.”

Feminists believed America could not truly be equal if women continued to face discrimination in everyday life.

Key Leaders

Betty Friedan

Betty Friedan was one of the most influential leaders of second-wave feminism. Her book The Feminine Mystique criticized traditional expectations for women.

Friedan later helped found the National Organization for Women (NOW) in 1966.

Gloria Steinem

Gloria Steinem became one of the movement’s most visible activists and writers. She co-founded Ms. Magazine in 1971 and advocated for equality in politics, careers, and education.

“Women are not a minority. We are half the people.”

Fannie Lou Hamer

Fannie Lou Hamer was a civil rights activist who also fought for voting rights and equality for Black women. She became nationally known after speaking at the 1964 Democratic National Convention.

Key Events

1960 – Approval of Birth Control Pills

The approval of birth control gave women greater control over family planning and careers. It became a major step toward reproductive freedom.

1963 – Equal Pay Act

The Equal Pay Act prohibited employers from paying women less than men for equal work under similar conditions.

1964 – Civil Rights Act

The Civil Rights Act banned discrimination based on race, religion, national origin, and sex. This law became one of the most important legal victories for women’s rights.

1966 – National Organization for Women (NOW)

Betty Friedan and other activists founded NOW after the government failed to effectively address discrimination against women.

“To bring women into full participation in the mainstream of American society.”

This mission statement from NOW served as an important primary source document because it clearly explained the organization’s goals and vision for equality.

1972 – Title IX

Title IX prohibited sex discrimination in schools and educational programs. It greatly expanded opportunities for women in academics and athletics.

Famous Speeches

Fannie Lou Hamer

Speech: Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party Speech (1964)

Hamer testified before the Democratic National Convention Credentials Committee about the violence and voter suppression Black Americans faced in Mississippi.

“I’m sick and tired of being sick and tired.”

This speech became one of the most powerful primary source documents of the civil rights era. It exposed racial injustice to a national audience and increased support for voting rights reform.

Betty Friedan

Speech: The Necessity of a Women’s Movement

Friedan gave speeches across the country discussing the frustrations many women experienced being limited to domestic roles.

“Women can no longer be satisfied with lives limited only to the home.”

These speeches helped inspire second-wave feminism and encouraged women to seek greater equality in careers, education, and politics.

Impact on Society

The Women’s Movement permanently changed American society. Women gained greater opportunities in education, athletics, healthcare, business, and government.

The movement also increased awareness about issues such as workplace discrimination, domestic violence, and political representation.

Many of the rights and opportunities women experience today were achieved because of the activism and protests of the women’s movement during the 1960s and 1970s.
