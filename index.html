Who Were They?

The Women’s Movement, also known as the second wave of feminism, took place primarily from the 1960s through the 1980s. Feminists fought for equal rights in the workplace, education, politics, and society. Women were often viewed as inferior to men and were expected to stay at home rather than pursue careers.

Activists organized protests, formed advocacy groups, and demanded legal reforms. Their work created many of the rights and opportunities women experience today.

“The problem that has no name” — Betty Friedan, The Feminine Mystique (1963)

This quote became one of the most famous ideas of the movement. Friedan described the unhappiness many women felt being limited to the role of housewife despite being told they should feel fulfilled. Her book inspired thousands of women to speak out and demand change.

Goals of the Movement

  • Equal pay for equal work
  • Equal opportunities in careers and education
  • Protection against discrimination based on sex
  • Reproductive rights and access to birth control
  • Greater political representation for women
  • Better treatment and equality for women of color

Women were commonly viewed as secretaries or assistants instead of professionals such as doctors or lawyers. In 1960, only 20 of the 537 members of Congress were women, despite women making up nearly half of the U.S. population.

Women of color faced even harsher discrimination. Black women were often paid less both because they were women and because of racial discrimination. Nearly 60% of employed Black women worked in service jobs such as maids, cooks, and laundresses.

“There can be no liberation for half the human race.”

Feminists believed society could not truly be fair if women were denied equal opportunities and respect.

Key Leaders

Betty Friedan

Betty Friedan wrote The Feminine Mystique, which challenged traditional expectations for women. She later founded the National Organization for Women (NOW), one of the most influential feminist organizations in America.

Elizabeth Cady Stanton

Elizabeth Cady Stanton was an important early activist for women’s rights and helped inspire later feminist movements. She fought for voting rights and equality for women in society.

Gloria Steinem

Gloria Steinem co-founded Ms. Magazine in 1971. She became one of the movement’s most recognizable voices and advocated for equality for all women, including women of color.

“Women are not a minority. We are half the people.”

This statement reflected Steinem’s belief that women deserved equal treatment because they represented half of society.

Key Events

1960 – Approval of Birth Control Pills

The approval of birth control gave women greater control over their reproductive lives and careers. By 1965, nearly 40% of young married women were using the pill.

1964 – Civil Rights Act

The Civil Rights Act banned discrimination based on race, religion, national origin, and sex. This law became one of the most important legal victories for women’s rights.

1966 – National Organization for Women (NOW)

Betty Friedan and other activists founded NOW after the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission failed to address discrimination against women.

“To bring women into full participation in the mainstream of American society.”

This mission statement from NOW became a guiding principle for the women’s movement and served as a primary source showing the organization’s goals.

1972 – Title IX

Title IX prohibited sex discrimination in schools and educational programs. It greatly expanded opportunities for women in academics and sports.

Equal Pay Act

The Equal Pay Act prohibited employers from paying women less than men for equal work under similar conditions.

Impact on Society

The Women’s Movement changed American society permanently. Women gained more opportunities in education, politics, healthcare, athletics, and business.

Modern careers such as healthcare support, office administration, and leadership positions are far more accessible to women today because of the work done by feminist activists in the 1960s and 1970s.

The movement also inspired future activism for equal rights and increased awareness of issues such as domestic violence, workplace discrimination, and representation in government.
